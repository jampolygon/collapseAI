# Survival priorities (rule of threes)
category: Survival
source: CollapseAI Essentials

A person can usually survive about:
- 3 minutes without air
- 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather (cold rain, extreme heat)
- 3 days without water
- 3 weeks without food

So the order of priorities is: immediate danger and first aid, then shelter, then water, then signaling for rescue, then food.

Stay calm. Panic wastes energy and leads to bad decisions. Stop, think, observe, plan.

# Making water safe to drink
category: Water
source: WHO / CDC household water treatment guidance

Unsafe water causes diarrhea, cholera and typhoid. Treat all water from rivers, wells, rain barrels and floods.

1. If cloudy, let it settle, then pour it through a clean cloth to remove dirt.
2. Then disinfect with one of these methods:

Boiling (best): bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute. Let it cool, keep it covered.

Bleach: use plain, unscented household bleach (5–6%). Add 2 drops per liter of clear water (4 drops per liter if cloudy or cold). Stir and wait 30 minutes. It should smell slightly of chlorine.

Solar disinfection (SODIS): fill clear plastic (PET) bottles of 2 liters or less with clear water. Lay them in full sunlight for 6 hours (2 days if very cloudy).

Water purification tablets: follow the package instructions.

Store treated water in clean, covered containers. Never drink flood water, even after boiling, if it may contain chemicals or fuel.

# Making fire
category: Fire
source: Based on US Army Survival Manual FM 21-76 (public domain)

Fire needs three things: heat, fuel and air.

Collect three kinds of fuel before you start:
- Tinder: very fine dry material that catches a spark: dry grass, coconut husk fibers, cotton, paper, wood shavings, dry leaves.
- Kindling: small dry sticks, pencil-thick.
- Fuel wood: thicker dry wood, wrist-thick and bigger.

Dead wood that snaps cleanly is dry. In wet conditions, split wood to reach the dry center, and make feather sticks (thin curls shaved on a stick).

Build a small teepee of kindling over the tinder, light the tinder, and blow gently at the base. Add bigger fuel slowly.

Ways to light it: matches, lighter, ferro rod, a 9V battery or flashlight batteries touched to steel wool, or a magnifying glass in strong sun.

Safety: never use fire in a closed room or tent (carbon monoxide can kill). Clear the ground around the fire. Keep water nearby.

# Emergency shelter
category: Shelter
source: Based on US Army Survival Manual FM 21-76 (public domain)

A shelter protects from rain, wind, sun and cold, and lets you rest.

Choosing a place: high ground away from floods and rivers, not under dead trees or loose rocks, close to water and materials, visible for rescue.

Quick shelters:
- Tarp or plastic sheet lean-to: tie a rope between two trees, drape the sheet over it, and pin the edges down. Slope it so rain runs off.
- Debris shelter: a long pole on a support, branches leaned on both sides, covered with a thick layer of leaves.

Insulate yourself from the ground with leaves, cardboard or a mat. You lose more heat to wet ground than to air.

In typhoon areas, a sturdy concrete building is safer than any improvised shelter.

# Signaling for rescue
category: Signals
source: Based on US Army Survival Manual FM 21-76 (public domain)

Three of anything is a distress signal: three fires, three whistle blasts, three flashes.

SOS in Morse code: three short, three long, three short (··· ––– ···). Use a flashlight, mirror or whistle.

Ground-to-air signals (make them large, at least 3 m, in contrasting colors):
- V: require assistance
- X: require medical assistance
- Arrow: proceeding in this direction

Signal mirror: reflect sunlight toward aircraft or ships; flashes can be seen many kilometers away.

Smoke: green leaves on a fire make white smoke (good against dark ground); rubber or oil makes black smoke (good against snow or light ground).

Brightly colored cloth on high ground or rooftops helps rescuers find you after floods.

# Finding direction without a compass
category: Navigation
source: Based on US Army Survival Manual FM 21-76 (public domain)

Sun: it rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west.

Shadow-stick method (works anywhere):
1. Push a stick upright into flat ground.
2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a stone.
3. Wait 15 minutes and mark the new shadow tip.
4. A line from the first mark to the second points roughly east. Standing with the first mark on your left and the second on your right, you face north.

At night: in the Philippines, Polaris (the North Star) is low above the northern horizon. Find the Big Dipper; the two stars at the end of its "cup" point to Polaris.

Use landmarks: note rivers, coastlines, mountains and roads. Rivers usually lead to towns and the sea.
